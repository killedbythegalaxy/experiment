## Key Question 1 — Scope of the job: how many animations does one fighting-game character actually need?

### Takeaway
No studio has published a per-character animation count for Street Fighter 6, Tekken 8, Guilty Gear Strive or Mortal Kombat 1 — I searched specifically for developer interviews and datamines and found none. The only hard, primary-ish numbers in the genre are 2D *frame* counts (Skullgirls: 11,515 frames across 8 characters = ~1,439/character, a Guinness record; 1,200–2,300 hand-drawn frames per character per the devs), and a widely reused community breakdown of a Street Fighter II-class character at ~69 distinct animations. Vendor estimates for a "fully featured" 3D fighter character cluster at 200–500 distinct clips. A deliberately scoped indie 3D fighter is realistically 100–180 clips ≈ 2–4 minutes of animation per character.

### Cited Findings
- A gamedev breakdown of a Street Fighter II-style character counts ~8 movement/defence animations, 48 attack animations (six attack buttons across movement states), 2 throws, 8 hit reactions and 3 specials = **~69 animations per character**, ~828 for a twelve-character cast — [Ask a Game Dev](https://askagamedev.tumblr.com/post/154472930146/hey-dev-i-was-wondering-about-something-when)
- A vendor/studio guide puts a **fully featured fighting-game character at 200–500 individual animations**, including idles, movement and attack sets — [Kevuru Games](https://kevurugames.com/blog/how-to-animate-a-fighting-game/) (vendor blog; single source for this figure)
- A combat-animation guide says a **minimal viable combat set is ~30–50 animations** — but it is written for action-RPG/melee combat, not fighting games, so it understates fighting-game needs — [MoCap Online, "Combat Animation for Games"](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/combat-animation-game-dev-guide)
- **Skullgirls** holds the Guinness World Record for "most frames of animation per character in a videogame": **11,515 frames used by its eight playable characters** — [Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/105505-most-frames-of-animation-per-character-in-a-videogame)
- Skullgirls developers: **each character contains between 1,200 and 2,300 individual hand-drawn frames** — [Lab Zero / Steam Community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/245170/discussions/0/792924952254858057/); see also the studio's own animation write-up — [skullgirls.com](https://skullgirls.com/2011/04/on-animation-in-skullgirls/)
- *The King of Fighters XII*: roughly **400–600 frames of fighting animation per character**; *BlazBlue*: roughly **1,000 hand-drawn frames per character** — [Kevuru Games](https://kevurugames.com/blog/how-to-animate-a-fighting-game/) (secondary)
- For 2D, "one animation consists of 5 to 30+ frames, depending on game, character" — i.e. the 1,400-frame figure maps to roughly 50–150 distinct animations — [Kevuru Games](https://kevurugames.com/blog/how-to-animate-a-fighting-game/)
- *Guilty Gear Xrd* rigs averaged **~500 bones per character**, built so 3D models could be posed frame-by-frame like hand-drawn art — i.e. the 3D-cel pipeline deliberately re-imports 2D labour costs — [Kevuru Games](https://kevurugames.com/blog/how-to-animate-a-fighting-game/)
- 3D fighters (Tekken, Virtua Fighter, Dead or Alive) are described as easier for small teams partly because **some animations can be re-used across characters** — [Andrea Jens, "I wanna make a fighting game!"](https://andrea-jens.medium.com/i-wanna-make-a-fighting-game-a-practical-guide-for-beginners-part-1-2021-update-955a4672eea5)
- Outsourcing studios scope by character tier rather than per-clip: **~90–130 hours per "hero" character vs 40–60 hours for standard units** — [vsquad.art animation estimate](https://vsquad.art/blog/cases/game-animation-estimate-character-tiers/) (vendor)
- Roster sizes for context: SF6 launched with 18 playable fighters, with DLC seasons planned to reach 34 — [Street Fighter Wiki](https://streetfighter.fandom.com/wiki/Street_Fighter_6); a 2026 listing counts 34 characters (31 main + 3 guests) — [Altar of Gaming](https://altarofgaming.com/game/street-fighter-6/characters/). Tekken 8 producer Michael Murray refers to "each of the 32 characters" in character episodes — [Bandai Namco interview](https://en.bandainamcoent.eu/tekken/news/tekken-8-interview-katsuhiro-harada-and-michael-murray). Guilty Gear Strive launched with 15 characters — [PlayStation store page](https://www.playstation.com/en-sa/games/guilty-gear-strive)
- Tekken 8's animation workload was split across multiple teams, with Maya for modelling and MotionBuilder for animation, and all characters rebuilt from scratch in UE5 — the only published production detail I found, and it contains no counts — [Autodesk case study, 28 Jan 2025](https://blogs.autodesk.com/media-and-entertainment/2025/01/28/tekken-8-legacy-meets-innovation-with-the-combined-power-of-maya-and-unreal-engine/)

### Inferences
- **Working scope for a solo dev's "20-move fighter" (my build-up, not a cited figure):** 20 special/command moves + 18 normals (5 standing / 5 crouching / 5 jumping / 3 command) + locomotion (idle, walk f/b, dash f/b, crouch in/out/idle, jump up/f/b + land) ≈ 13 + block high/low + blockstun high/low + 6–8 hit reactions + knockdown/getup/wakeup ≈ 12 + 2 throws (whiff/hit/thrown-victim) ≈ 6 + 2 supers + intro + round win + match win + loss/timeout ≈ 5. **Total ≈ 110–150 clips.** That sits between the 69-clip SF2 baseline and the 200–500 "fully featured" vendor figure, and is the number every cost line below is computed against.
- **Seconds of animation:** fighting-game clips are short — most moves run 0.3–1.5 s (roughly 20–90 frames at 60fps), with intros/supers/win poses at 2–5 s. 130 clips averaging ~1.0 s plus ~6 long clips at ~3.5 s ⇒ **≈ 150–200 seconds (2.5–3.5 minutes) of finished animation per character.** This is the single most important reframing in the whole analysis: the job is not "make a movie", it is "make ~3 minutes of extremely dense, frame-exact motion", and per-second rates therefore mislead badly because the setup/polish cost per clip dominates the duration cost.
- Fighting-game animation is the worst possible fit for mocap-style per-second economics and the best possible fit for hand-keyed/pose-to-pose work, because the value is in the *frame counts* (startup/active/recovery) that gameplay demands, not in motion realism. Any AI or mocap route must therefore be budgeted as "reference generation + full manual retiming", not as "finished animation".

### Gaps
- **No published per-character animation counts for SF6, Tekken 8, GG Strive or MK1.** Searches for developer interviews and datamines returned roster sizes and PR quotes only. The report should say this plainly rather than cite a number. Possible unexplored routes: Dustloop/SuperCombo frame-data move counts as a proxy; Tekken movelists (Tekken characters commonly have 100+ listed command moves, which would push clip counts far above 500 — I did not find a citable source for this and have not asserted it).
- No indie-fighter developer has published an animation-count postmortem that I could find.

## Key Question 2 — Cost comparison with 2026 numbers

### Takeaway
For ~130 clips on one character, the four routes price out roughly as: **freelance animator $16k–100k**; **optical mocap stage day + cleanup $5k–15k+ for a 200-clip combat set**; **buy an inertial suit and shoot it yourself $3k–6k capital + weeks of your own cleanup**; **AI video mocap $20–60/month of service fees plus the same weeks of cleanup**; **pre-made animation packs $80–300 total, at $0.35–$3 per clip**. The decisive variable in every route except packs is cleanup labour, which the industry rule of thumb puts at 3–5× recorded time; if you value your own time at $0, AI-video or suit capture is nearly free in cash and expensive in months.

### Cited Findings — (a) Hiring a freelance 3D animator
- Per-animation game-asset rates: **simple rigged idle/walk cycle $150–$500; complex combat or spell animation $500–$1,500; frame-by-frame character cycles $1,000–$3,000** — [goLance animator hourly rate guide, 2026](https://golance.com/hiring/best-freelance-animators-hourly-rate) (marketplace/vendor guide)
- A competing guide gives much lower per-animation figures: **basic idle/walk $25–$100; complex combat or transformation $100–$500+** — [FreelanceNation](https://freelancenation.com/blog/cost-to-hire-an-animator). The ~5× spread between these two sources is unresolved and should be reported as a spread, not averaged.
- Hourly by seniority: **junior $20–$40/hr, mid $40–$75/hr, senior $75–$130/hr** — [goLance, 2026](https://golance.com/hiring/best-freelance-animators-hourly-rate); a second 2026 guide gives **junior $25–$50/hr and senior game-portfolio specialists $100–$200/hr+** — [Biztoolkit, "3D Artist & Animator Freelance Rates in 2026"](https://www.biztoolkit.co/post/3d-artist-animator-freelance-rates-in-2026)
- **Marketplace reality is lower than the guides:** Upwork animator rates typically **$20–$38/hr, median $25/hr** — [Sidestackers rate page](https://www.sidestackers.com/rates/animator)
- Regional rate cards: **USA/Canada $100–$200/hr; Eastern Europe $25–$60/hr; India/South Asia $20–$50/hr** — [Biztoolkit 2026](https://www.biztoolkit.co/post/3d-artist-animator-freelance-rates-in-2026)
- Pricing-model norms: **per asset $100–$1,000+; per frame $5–$25; project flat fee $500–$10,000+** — [Biztoolkit 2026](https://www.biztoolkit.co/post/3d-artist-animator-freelance-rates-in-2026)
- Outsourcing studios quote by hours per character: **~90–130 h for a hero character, 40–60 h for standard** — [vsquad.art](https://vsquad.art/blog/cases/game-animation-estimate-character-tiers/)
- **Hard historical datapoint from an actual shipped fighting game (2D, but the only real one):** Lab Zero paid **$20 per frame for animation and clean-up ($10 for each)** on Skullgirls, with Squigly estimated at ~1,500 frames — [Steam Community / dev quote](https://steamcommunity.com/app/245170/discussions/0/792924952254858057/). Community breakdowns put a full added Skullgirls character at **~$150,000** including crowdfunding fees, with **$30,000** of that line-itemed for animation and clean-up contracting — [NeoGAF breakdown](https://www.neogaf.com/threads/the-exact-cost-and-breakdown-of-adding-a-character-to-skullgirls-150k.515688/page-4); [2dwillneverdie, "How much do sprites cost?"](https://2dwillneverdie.com/blog/how-much-do-sprites-cost/) (both secondary/community, not official accounting)
- A vendor estimates **$20,000–$50,000 to build a complete combat set for one weapon archetype from scratch** — [MoCap Online pipeline guide](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/game-animation-pipeline-guide) (the vendor sells packs, so this figure is self-serving)

### Inferences
- **Costed scenarios for one character, ~130 clips (my computations from the cited rates — label as derived, not sourced):**
  | Route | Cash cost | Your time | Notes |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | US/Western freelancer, per-animation rates | **$40k–$100k+** (40 simple @ $300 + 90 combat @ $800 ≈ $84k) | low | goLance rate card; prohibitive for a solo dev |
  | Eastern-European freelancer, hourly | **$16k–$39k** (130 clips × 3–5 h × $25–60/hr) | low | Biztoolkit regional card; still a funded-project number |
  | Upwork median hourly | **$10k–$20k** (130 × 3–5 h × $25/hr median) | low | realistic floor for "hire someone", quality variance high |
  | Optical stage, half/full day + cleanup | **$5k–$15k+** for a ~200-clip set | moderate | MoCap Online's own worked example |
  | Own inertial suit (Rokoko Indie Pro) + friend who does martial arts | **$3.0k–$4.4k** hardware + **$120–$600/yr** software | **100–300 h** cleanup (3–5× record time) | capital survives across all characters, so per-character cost collapses after character #1 |
  | AI video mocap (Rokoko Plus annual) | **$240/yr** ($20/mo; 3,000 s/mo of video is ~20× what one character needs) | **100–300 h** cleanup | cheapest cash route that still yields *your* motion |
  | Pre-made packs | **$80–$300 total** | **20–60 h** retarget + retime | $0.35/clip for Kubold's 230 animations is the lowest per-clip figure available anywhere |
- **The AI route's cash cost is trivially small and essentially irrelevant to the decision.** A year of Rokoko Plus ($240) or Move AI Starter (~$216/yr) is less than one day of a mid-tier optical stage. The real cost of every non-pack route is 100–300 hours of cleanup and retiming at 3–5× record time, which at even $25/hr of opportunity cost is **$2,500–$7,500 of implicit labour per character** — more than the hardware.
- **Per-second rate cards are the wrong unit for this genre.** At ~150–200 s of finished animation per character, a "$20/second" quote implies $3,000–4,000/character, which is wildly below what the per-animation rate cards imply ($40k–100k) — because a fighting-game second contains 5–10× the keyframe density of a cinematic second. Expect animators quoting per-second to re-quote upward once they see a frame-data spec.
- **Packs are the only route whose economics work for a solo dev at a zero-funding start**, and the Kubold pack's 230 clips against a 110–150-clip need means a single $80 purchase can nominally cover a whole character — with the caveat that generic mocap-derived clips will not have fighting-game-legal startup/active/recovery frames without retiming.

## Key Question 3 — Hardware for local/open-source AI, and cloud vs. buy

### Takeaway
The relevant open video-to-motion models are 24 GB-class workloads in practice (GVHMR is community-tested on an RTX 3090/4090; a Mac port peaked at 8.4 GB), which means a used RTX 3090/4090 or a new 5090 covers it — but cloud rental at ~$0.69–$0.99/hr for a 5090 means **buying hardware only pays back after roughly 2,500–4,000 GPU-hours**, which almost no solo dev will reach for this task. For this workload specifically, cloud is clearly cheaper.

### Cited Findings
- **GVHMR** (world-grounded human motion recovery from monocular video, ZJU3DV) publishes speed, not VRAM: the network, excluding preprocessing (2D tracking, feature extraction, relative camera rotation estimation), **takes 280 ms to process a 1,430-frame (~45 s) video on an RTX 4090** — [arXiv:2409.06662](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06662v1); [project page](https://zju3dv.github.io/gvhmr/); [ACM SIGGRAPH Asia 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3680528.3687565)
- A community GVHMR tutorial lists the requirement as **an NVIDIA GPU with 24 GB (tested on an RTX 3090)** — [GVHMR-tutorial](https://github.com/airecode/GVHMR-tutorial); a Patreon setup guide reports testing on **RTX 3090 24 GB + 48 GB system RAM**, adding "it should run on lower settings, but I can't say what is the minimum" — [Patreon guide](https://patreon.com/posts/mocap-using-pre-143703721)
- **The only measured peak-memory figure I found:** an Apple-Silicon port of GVHMR reports **76 s video-to-motion (3.9 fps), peak 8.4 GB on an M1 Max** for a 300-frame 1600×1000 clip — [atelier PR #199](https://github.com/fontanierh/atelier/pull/199). This strongly suggests the real VRAM floor is well under 24 GB and the "24 GB" figure is a comfort recommendation, not a hard requirement.
- **WHAM:** no VRAM figure found. It requires a DPVO SLAM build (a setup dependency, not a memory figure) and ~2.0 s for the core network — [search-surfaced via GVHMR comparisons](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06662v1)
- **RTX 5090 street price, 2026:** sources disagree widely. - Physics-based RL motion training (DeepMimic-style / AMP) is the one sub-workload with genuinely large compute appetite and is also the one whose output quality is least predictable — for a solo dev shipping a 20-move fighter it is a research project, not a pipeline.

## Key Question 4 — Legal: copying motion, likeness, dataset licences, AI disclosure

### Takeaway
**Motion and short move sequences are the weakest form of IP in this whole stack.** The U.S. Copyright Office position (Circular 52) is that individual movements, dance steps, short routines "consisting of only a few movements or steps", athletic movements, feats of physical skill and ordinary motor activities are **not copyrightable** — which covers essentially everything a fighting-game move is. The Ninth Circuit's *Hanagami v. Epic* (Nov 2023) cut against over-broad dismissal by holding that choreography must be assessed as a *selection and arrangement*, not reduced to unprotectable poses — and the case then **settled in Feb 2024 without a merits ruling**, as did all four Fortnite emote suits in 2019. The real exposures for an AI-assisted fighting game are **(1) right of publicity** if the source video shows an identifiable person, **(2) the source video's own copyright** (a film fight scene is an audiovisual work even if the choreography isn't protected), and **(3) non-commercial dataset licences — AMASS being the fatal one**, which explicitly prohibits incorporation in a commercial product. Steam requires AI disclosure under rules significantly rewritten on **16 January 2026**.

### Cited Findings — (a) Is motion/choreography copyrightable?
**Primary source — U.S. Copyright Office, Circular 52, "Copyright Registration of Choreography and Pantomime"** — [copyright.gov/circs/circ52.pdf](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ52.pdf):
- Statutory basis: §102(a)(4) protects "pantomimes and choreographic works" created after 1 January 1978 and fixed in a tangible medium.
- "Choreography and pantomimes consisting of **ordinary motor activities, social dances, commonplace movements or gestures, or athletic movements may lack a sufficient amount of authorship to qualify for copyright protection.**"
- "**Individual movements or dance steps by themselves are not copyrightable**, such as the basic waltz step, the hustle step, the grapevine, or the second position in classical ballet. The U.S. Copyright Office **cannot register short dance routines consisting of only a few movements or steps** with minor linear or spatial variations, **even if a routine is novel or distinctive**."
- Expressly unregistrable examples include "**a celebratory end zone dance move or athletic victory gesture**", yoga positions, and groups spelling letters with their arms.
- "Functional physical movements, **feats of physical skill or dexterity**, and ordinary motor activities — in and of themselves — are not eligible for registration as choreography." Listed examples: general exercise routines, **"athletic activities, such as a new tennis swing, a golf swing, or a unique slam-dunk maneuver"**, feats of physical skill or dexterity, skateboarding or snowboarding tricks, yoga poses and sequences, **"a compilation of any of the above"**.
- "Registrable choreographic works are **typically intended to be executed by skilled performers before an audience**", whereas social dances/simple routines are not registrable "**even if they contain a substantial amount of creative expression**".
- Fixation requires detail "sufficient to permit the work to be performed in a consistent and uniform manner"; acceptable formats include **Labanotation/Benesh notation, video recordings, textual descriptions, photographs or drawings**.
- **"Choreographic works and pantomimes must be intended for execution by humans."** Routines "created for robots, machines, or other inanimate objects to perform" are **not copyrightable as choreography** — a notable point for motion data driving a game character.
- Derivative choreography requires independently created new material with sufficient creativity; "**simply making minor changes or trivial additions to a preexisting choreographic work... would not satisfy this requirement.**"

**Hanagami v. Epic Games:**
- District court (Aug 2022, Judge Stephen V. Wilson) dismissed, holding Hanagami lacked protection for individual "poses" and that the allegedly copied portion was closer to an uncopyrightable "short" routine — [Loeb & Loeb, 2022](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2022/09/hanagami-v-epic-games-inc)
- **Ninth Circuit, 1 November 2023: reversed.** The panel held the district court erred in reducing the choreographic work to a series of unprotectable individual poses and in finding the copied portion too short/simple to be protectable; choreography, like other copyrightable material, "comprises various elements that are unprotectable in isolation but are protectable as a collective arrangement and selection by the choreographer" — [opinion text](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/115395594.html); [Arnold & Porter advisory](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2023/11/hanagami-v-epic-games-the-ninth-circuit-clarifies); [Loeb & Loeb, Nov 2023](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2023/11/hanagami-v-epic-games); [Copyright Lately](https://copyrightlately.com/choreography-copyright-gets-its-due-in-the-ninth-circuit/); described as a first-of-its-kind ruling for choreography — [MSK](https://www.msk.com/newsroom-alerts-choreography-copyright-case)
- **February 2024: the case ended without a merits ruling.** Epic and Hanagami jointly asked a Los Angeles federal judge to dismiss the suit, which had been set for trial in May; **settlement terms were not made public** — [Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/business/legal/fortnite-dance-choreographer-ends-epic-games-copyright-lawsuit-1235607331/)
- **The 2018–2019 Fortnite emote suits (2 Milly, Alfonso Ribeiro, Backpack Kid, Orange Shirt Kid) all ended in voluntary dismissals without prejudice in March 2019**, for procedural reasons — the Supreme Court's *Fourth Estate* decision required plaintiffs to wait for the Copyright Office to act on registration — [Fox Rothschild](https://payorplay.foxrothschild.com/2019/03/articles/litigation-and-statutory-developments/celebrity-plaintiffs-alleging-fortnite-publisher-stole-their-dance-moves-face-the-music-and-dismiss-claims/); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/fortnite-dance-lawsuits-dropped-at-least-for-now-1833208400); [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/business-news/fortnite-legal-dance-battles-paused-supreme-court-ruling-1193167/). Plaintiffs' counsel (Pierce Bainbridge) confirmed the moves were "purely procedural"; the dismissals can be refiled.
- Epic's own litigating position, via counsel Dale Cendali (Feb 2019): the claim was "fundamentally at odds with free speech principles", and **"no one can own a dance step"** — [MassivelyOP](https://massivelyop.com/2019/02/13/epic-games-files-to-dismiss-rapper-2-millys-fortnite-dance-lawsuit/); [Euronews/AP coverage](https://www.euronews.com/2019/02/13/fortnite-maker-pushes-back-against-copyright-lawsuit-no-one-can-n971121)

### Cited Findings — (b) Right of publicity / likeness
- **Keller v. Electronic Arts (9th Cir. 2013):** EA's unauthorised use of a former college football player's likeness was **not, as a matter of law, protected by the First Amendment**; the transformative-use test asks whether the work "adds significant creative elements beyond a mere likeness" — [ABA Litigation Section](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/committees/intellectual-property/articles/2014/right-publicity-video-games-plays-hardball-with-first-amendment-part-2/); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/use-athlete-s-likeness-video-game-not-protected-first-amendment)
- **Hart v. Electronic Arts (3d Cir. 2013):** same result in a different circuit; the game used no name but used a digital avatar recreating the player's "exact physical, biographical, and career statistics" — [Temple Law Review case note](https://www.templelawreview.org/its-in-the-game-hart-v-electronic-arts-inc-rejects-video-game-producers-first-amendment-defense-to-college-athletes-right-of-publicity-claim/)
- **No Doubt v. Activision (Cal. Ct. App.):** the band's publicity rights were violated when avatars were used **beyond the scope of the licence** — and critically, the band had "posed for cameras so Activision could create detailed computer-generated avatars", making this the closest analogue to a captured performer — [ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/committees/intellectual-property/articles/2014/right-publicity-video-games-plays-hardball-with-first-amendment-part-2/)
- **Noriega v. Activision** failed where the depiction was a "generalized, satirical archetype rather than a specific likeness" — failing the recognizability requirement — and **Lohan v. Take-Two** is analysed on the same recognizability axis, with commentary noting that **"if the output is recognizable as a particular person and is used in trade, the rendering technology is irrelevant"** — [Outside IP Counsel on Lohan](https://outsideipcounsel.com/blog/lohan-v-take-two-avatar-portrait-recognizability/)
- **Champion v. Take-Two (N.Y. Sup. Ct.):** a basketball entertainer's NBA 2K18 avatar claim was dismissed, but the court **confirmed New York's publicity statute can reach video-game avatars** — [CDAS](https://cdas.com/videogame-rightofpublicity/)
- Practitioner framing of the rule for game devs — [Game Developer, "Can I portray a real person in my game?"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/can-i-portray-a-real-person-in-my-game-) and [Game Developer, "Right of Publicity in Video Games"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/right-of-publicity-in-video-games---how-you-can-legally-include-a-celebrity-in-your-game)

### Cited Findings — (c) Dataset licences
- **AMASS licence (primary):** grants "you (Licensee) personally a single-user, non-exclusive, non-transferable, free of charge right" for **non-commercial scientific research, education, or artistic projects** — [amass/LICENSE.txt](https://github.com/nghorbani/amass/blob/master/LICENSE.txt)
- A mirror of the same licence spells out that **"any use for commercial purposes is prohibited. This includes, without limitation, incorporation in a commercial product, use in a commercial service, or production of other artefacts for commercial purposes"** — [HuggingFace mirror of AMASS licence](https://huggingface.co/datasets/fleaven/Retargeted_AMASS_for_bxi_elf2/blob/1ae0e4e0f65602ff445e494224a16fb0d2b3cf80/license.txt)
- Commercial rights require a separate deal: **"for commercial licensing, please contact ps-licensing@tue.mpg.de"** — [AMASS repo](https://github.com/nghorbani/amass)
- **Derived datasets inherit the restriction:** Motion-X and HumanML3D both state AMASS is "released for academic research only and it is free to researchers from educational or research institutes for non-commercial purposes" — [Motion-X project page](https://motion-x-dataset.github.io/); [Motion-X licence PDF](https://motion-x-dataset.github.io/static/license/Motion-X%20License.pdf); [arXiv:2307.00818](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.00818)
- **Trap for the unwary:** a Kaggle repackaging of AMASS SMPL+H is labelled **CC0 and tagged for games** — that label does not override the upstream Max Planck terms — [Kaggle AMASS SMPL+H](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/mrriandmstique/amass-smpl-h-g/data)
- A comparative survey of motion-dataset licensing for practitioners — [Uthana, "3D Human Motion Datasets Compared"](https://uthana.com/resources/human-motion-datasets)

### Cited Findings — (d) Steam / platform AI disclosure
- **Valve significantly rewrote Steam's AI disclosure rules; the change was reported on 16 January 2026** (one outlet dates it 17 January) — [Game Developer, 16 Jan 2026](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/valve-tweaks-and-clarifies-ai-disclosure-rules-for-steam); [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/valve-has-significantly-rewritten-steams-rules-for-how-developers-much-disclose-ai-use); [Generation Amiga, 17 Jan 2026](https://www.generationamiga.com/2026/01/17/valve-rewrites-steams-ai-disclosure-rules-for-developers/). The update was spotted by GameDiscoverCo's Simon Carless.
- **Two disclosure categories:** "AI to generate content for the game" and "AI content generated during gameplay". For development-time generation, the disclosure covers the game **and associated material such as marketing assets or the store page**, and developers **must fill out a text box describing their tool use**. For runtime generation, developers **check a box** if the game itself produces AI-generated images, text or other content — [Game Developer, 16 Jan 2026](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/valve-tweaks-and-clarifies-ai-disclosure-rules-for-steam)
- **Workflow/efficiency tools are exempt:** developers do not have to disclose "AI powered tools" used for workflow purposes such as code-helper software — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/valve-tweaks-and-clarifies-ai-disclosure-rules-for-steam); secondary coverage puts it as "efficiency tools (code assistants, meeting transcription, ideation) no longer trigger store disclosures" — [tbreak](https://tbreak.com/steam-ai-disclosure-policy-update-developers/)
- Developers must **verify that pre-generated AI content does not include illegal or infringing material**, and must **describe guardrails** preventing illegal live-generated output — [tbreak](https://tbreak.com/steam-ai-disclosure-policy-update-developers/); [PCGamesN on AI content reporting](https://www.pcgamesn.com/steam/ai-content-reporting)
- Disclosures surface publicly on the store page in an **"AI Generated Content Disclosure"** section, and **players can report illegal content through the in-game overlay** — [tbreak](https://tbreak.com/steam-ai-disclosure-policy-update-developers/); [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/steam/ai-content-reporting); original 2024 framework coverage — [GeekWire, 2024](https://www.geekwire.com/2024/valve-software-reveals-new-rules-for-ai-powered-game-development-on-steam/)
- **Conflict in the sources:** one guide calls the disclosure "voluntary", while others describe the questions as sitting in a **mandatory section of the Steamworks Content Survey** — [contradiction between](https://legalmoveslawfirm.com/steam-ai-policy/) [and](https://tbreak.com/steam-ai-disclosure-policy-update-developers/). Treat the survey as mandatory. One tracker claims AI disclosure now appears on **~20% of games on Steam** — [tech-insider.org](https://tech-insider.org/steam-ai-disclosure-2026/) (unverified SEO source)
- **I could not retrieve Valve's own Steamworks documentation text.** The URL partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/aidisclosure returned the Steamworks documentation home page rather than the policy; the closest navigation entry is "Content Survey" under Getting Started. **The report must therefore attribute the policy content to secondary coverage and direct readers to the Steamworks Content Survey for the operative text.**

### Inferences
- **The practical legal read on "can I mocap a movie fight scene and ship it?"** Circular 52 says the *movements* themselves — athletic movements, feats of physical skill, individual steps, short routines — are not registrable, and that a compilation of unprotectable movements may itself fail. *Hanagami* does not overturn that; it holds that a *longer, fixed, expressive choreographic composition* must be judged as a selection-and-arrangement rather than dismissed as poses. A fighting-game move (0.5 s, one strike) sits squarely in Circular 52's unprotectable zone; a recreated 30-second signature fight *sequence* with its distinctive ordering is where *Hanagami* risk starts. **The exposure is not usually choreography copyright — it is that the source video is itself a copyrighted audiovisual work, and that using it as training/extraction input is a separate, unsettled question; plus the performer's right of publicity.**
- **Right of publicity is the sharper risk than copyright** for AI video extraction. *Keller*, *Hart* and *No Doubt* collectively show that a recognisable person's digital embodiment in a game loses the transformative-use defence, and the *Lohan* commentary's point that "the rendering technology is irrelevant" means laundering a performance through a motion-extraction model does not help. Conversely, extracting pure skeletal motion from footage and applying it to a visually unrelated original character is the configuration most likely to fail the recognizability requirement that sank *Noriega* — i.e. **the safe harbour is "take the motion, discard everything identifying"**, and this is exactly what motion data does. This is an inference from the cited cases, not a holding.
- **AMASS is the single most dangerous dependency in the open-source motion-AI stack for a commercial game.** Most text-to-motion and motion-diffusion research models are trained on AMASS or AMASS-derived data (HumanML3D, Motion-X), and the licence prohibits "incorporation in a commercial product" and "production of other artefacts for commercial purposes" — language broad enough to arguably reach *generated output*, not just the data. A solo dev shipping on Steam who used an AMASS-trained model to generate shipped animations has an unresolved licence problem that no disclosure form fixes. The commercially clean alternatives are: your own captured motion, Mixamo (explicitly royalty-free for commercial games), licensed packs, and vendor services whose terms grant commercial use (Rokoko's Motion Library explicitly does).
- **Valve's January 2026 rewrite is favourable to this pipeline:** using an AI tool to *generate motion during development* is pre-generated content requiring a text-box disclosure, not a prohibition, and tool-assisted workflow is exempt. The "verify no infringing material" requirement is the clause that imports the AMASS/source-footage problem back into the store submission.

### Gaps
- **No primary Valve/Steamworks policy text retrieved** (see above). Console (Sony/Microsoft/Nintendo) and app-store AI disclosure requirements as of 2026: **I found nothing** — no searches returned platform-holder AI policies for consoles or mobile stores. This is a real gap in the brief's coverage.
- **No court decision anywhere on whether motion-capture data itself is copyrightable**, and no case brought by a motion-capture performer. The question is genuinely unresolved; both *Hanagami* and the emote suits ended without merits rulings, so there is **no binding precedent on whether a game move is protectable**.
- No information on whether *Hanagami*'s reasoning has been adopted outside the Ninth Circuit.
- SMPL body-model licence terms (separate from AMASS) not confirmed — relevant if SMPL meshes rather than just motion are involved.
- No 2025–2026 AI-training-specific disclosure requirement found beyond Steam's.

## Key Question 5 — Monetization reality

### Takeaway
The commercial evidence on indie fighters is thin and the estimates are wildly inconsistent, but the shape is clear: **the genre's indie commercial outcomes are modest**. Them's Fightin' Herds — arguably the most visible crowdfunded indie fighter — carries a third-party estimate of **~$2.36M gross / ~$696k net to the developer**, against a cost structure where Skullgirls (its lineage's predecessor) spent **~$150k per added character**. Pocket Bravery shows **738 reviews at 89% positive**. Fantasy Strike's equity crowdfund **raised $147,819 against a $500,000 goal** and the game now shows a **3-player 24-hour peak**. Against this, animation packs sell at $80–300 and a $80 pack of 230 fighting animations has 21 reviews on Fab — a small market, but one with near-zero marginal cost.

### Cited Findings
- **Them's Fightin' Herds:** a Steam revenue calculator estimates **~$2.36 million gross revenue since release, with ~$695,687 net to the developer**; PlayTracker gives an owner estimate of **44,760–134,280 based on reviews**, while other modelling implies 800,000–910,000 Steam owners — **the two methods disagree by roughly an order of magnitude and both are modelled, not official** — [Steam Revenue Calculator](https://steam-revenue-calculator.com/app/574980/them's-fightin'-herds); [PlayTracker](https://playtracker.net/insight/game/22275); [SteamDB app 574980](https://steamdb.info/app/574980/)
- **Pocket Bravery:** Steam price **$19.99**, discounted to **$11.99** on PlayStation; **89% positive from 738 reviews** — a small review count that does not indicate sales volume — [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1555150/Pocket_Bravery/); [Humble Indie Fighters Bundle listing](https://gamebundleguardian.com/bundle/humble-indie-fighters-bundle); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_Bravery). - **Cost side, the only real published figure in the genre:** adding one character to Skullgirls was broken down at **~$150,000** including crowdfunding fees, with **$30,000** line-itemed for animation and clean-up contracting at **$20/frame** — [NeoGAF](https://www.neogaf.com/threads/the-exact-cost-and-breakdown-of-adding-a-character-to-skullgirls-150k.515688/page-4); [2dwillneverdie](https://2dwillneverdie.com/blog/how-much-do-sprites-cost/); [Lab Zero dev quote](https://steamcommunity.com/app/245170/discussions/0/792924952254858057/)

Kubold publicly reported **$7,875 donated to charity** from one pack's sales over several years — [kubold.com blog, 21 Sep 2018](https://www.kubold.com/blog/2018/9/21/longsword-animset-pro-made-7875-for-charity-to-this-date), a rare published signal of actual pack revenue scale.
- Low-end sprite/asset supply: itch.io fighting-animation packs typically offer **8–12 animations per character** — [itch.io fighting animation assets](https://itch.io/game-assets/tag-animation/tag-fighting)

### Inferences
- **The unit economics of an indie fighter are brutal and well-documented by Skullgirls:** at $150k/character, even a 6-character roster is $900k, against a best-case third-party estimate of ~$696k net for the genre's most successful crowdfunded indie entry. **This is the strongest argument in the entire research for the AI/pack route: not that it produces better animation, but that it is the only way the per-character cost falls to a level where a solo dev's revenue expectations can cover it.**
- **Fighting games have an unusually hostile retention profile for indies** (Fantasy Strike's 3-player concurrent peak despite 200k owners and an IGN award), because the genre's value depends on matchmaking liquidity — a problem no amount of animation quality solves. A solo dev optimising for revenue should weigh single-player-heavy or asymmetric designs, or non-game products.
- **Selling animation packs is a plausible but small business**: Kubold's $7,875 charity figure from one pack over years, and 21 Fab reviews on a decade-old best-in-class fighting pack, suggest pack revenue is in the hundreds-to-low-thousands of dollars per year per pack — but marginal cost after production is near zero and the same motion library feeds both the pack and your own game. **The strongest monetisation structure available here is to build the motion library once, ship the fighter, and sell the library as a pack** — the asset is dual-use, and the Mixamo/Fab restriction that bites (no reselling raw files that came from someone else) does not apply to motion you captured yourself.
- Selling a *service* (capture-and-cleanup for other indies) at the cited cleanup rate of $75–$150/hr is, arithmetically, the highest-certainty revenue of the three options — it converts the same 100–300 hours of cleanup skill into $7,500–$45,000 of billable work instead of a speculative game launch.

- **No data at all** for Rushdown Revolt or Dong Dong Never Die; no Gamalytic or VG Insights figures reached.

## Key Question 6 — Fastest, cheapest realistic path for a solo dev in late 2026

### Takeaway
The recommendations I could source all point the same way and none of them is "use AI to generate finished animation": **buy a pack as the base layer, capture your own motion cheaply (AI video or a borrowed/rented inertial suit) only for the signature moves, and spend your scarce hours on retiming rather than on creation.** The practitioner consensus is that cleanup — not capture — is the bottleneck, at 3–5× recorded time, and that scope creep is the solo dev's main failure mode.

### Cited Findings
- **Cleanup is the cost centre:** "the industry rule of thumb is 3–5× the recorded time for cleaning up a mocap session", covering foot and hand planting, root-motion extraction, loop-point alignment, curve smoothing and secondary-motion polish — [MoCap Online pipeline guide](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/game-animation-pipeline-guide)
- **Buy, don't build, as the default indie route:** building a complete combat set for one weapon archetype from scratch is estimated at **$20,000–$50,000**, and "a comprehensive animation pack covers the majority of locomotion, action, and transition needs" — [MoCap Online pipeline guide](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/game-animation-pipeline-guide) (vendor, self-interested)
- **Free tooling is adequate:** Blender's Graph Editor for F-curve editing is "essential for mocap cleanup" and the workflow is "entirely viable for production", though retargeting is more manual than Maya/MotionBuilder (which runs ~$1,875/yr) — [MoCap Online software comparison, 2026](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/3d-animation-software-comparison)
- **Scope control over tool choice:** "a solo developer's main risk is scope creep... keeping the move list small and reusing a shared state machine will matter more than the choice of cleanup tool" — [MoCap Online pipeline guide](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/game-animation-pipeline-guide)
- **Choose 3D over 2D and exploit reuse:** 3D fighters are "easier for small teams, partly because you can re-use some animations for more than one character" — [Andrea Jens's practical beginner's guide](https://andrea-jens.medium.com/i-wanna-make-a-fighting-game-a-practical-guide-for-beginners-part-1-2021-update-955a4672eea5). The 2D route's published cost is $20/frame × 1,200–2,300 frames per character — [Lab Zero](https://steamcommunity.com/app/245170/discussions/0/792924952254858057/)
- **Fighting-specific AI tooling exists but is immature:** a 2026 community pipeline chains an LLM agent, video-to-motion and Mixamo retargeting with real mesh-vs-mesh collision checking in Blender, which the author argues suits "duels, fights, and paired interactions" — unproven, cleanup claims unverified — [baeseokjae.github.io, 2026](https://baeseokjae.github.io/posts/mixamo-llm-mocap-ai-agent-2026/)
- **Commercially clean motion sources for a shipped game:** Mixamo is royalty-free for commercial games including video games — [Adobe Mixamo FAQ](https://helpx.adobe.com/creative-cloud/faq/mixamo-faq.html); Rokoko's Motion Library animations may be "used in any of your projects, including commercial ones" — [rokoko.com/pricing](https://www.rokoko.com/pricing); Kubold's pack ships under the Unity Asset Store EULA Single Entity licence at $80 — [Unity Asset Store](https://marketplace.unity.com/packages/3d/animations/fighting-animset-pro-64666)
- **Martial-arts-specific capture guidance** exists from the same vendor — [MoCap Online, "Martial Arts Animation"](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/martial-arts-animation-fighting-styles)

### Inferences
- **The recommendation I would synthesise (derived, with costs from the cited rates), for one 20-move 3D fighter, ~130 clips:**
  1. **Base layer — $80–$300, ~1 weekend.** Buy Kubold Fighting Animset Pro ($80, 230 clips, Single Entity Unity EULA) and/or pull Mixamo (free, explicitly commercial-safe) for locomotion, blocks, hit reactions, knockdowns, getups. These are the ~60–70 clips nobody will ever praise you for hand-making. Retarget in Blender (free).
  2. **Signature layer — $0–$240/yr, 2–4 weeks.** Record your 20 special moves + 2 supers + intro/win poses on video (phone, 60fps, locked camera, or a friend who trains martial arts), run them through Rokoko Vision (Plus tier $20/mo annual = 3,000 s/month, ~20× what you need) or Move AI Starter (~$18/mo), and export FBX. Cash cost under $250 for the year; this is the only part of the character that must be yours.
  3. **Retiming layer — the actual work, 100–300 h.** Every clip gets fighting-game frame data imposed on it: startup/active/recovery windows, hold-on-contact frames, cancel windows. **No AI tool does this**, and it is the step that determines whether the game feels good. Budget 1–2 hours per gameplay-relevant clip.
  4. **Hardware — $0.** Try GVHMR/local models on the GPU you own first (an 8.4 GB measured peak suggests 12–16 GB suffices); if you need more, rent a 5090 at $0.69–$0.99/hr rather than buying at $2,400–$5,000. Break-even is ~2,500–3,600 GPU-hours, which this workload will never reach.
  5. **Legal hygiene — $0.** Avoid any AMASS-trained generative model in the shipped-asset path (non-commercial licence explicitly bars incorporation in a commercial product). Capture your own performer with a written release, or use Mixamo/licensed packs. Do not recreate a recognisable real person. Complete Steam's Content Survey AI disclosure honestly: development-time generation = pre-generated content, text-box description required; workflow tools exempt.
  - **Total cash: roughly $300–$600 for the first character** (pack + a year of an AI mocap subscription + miscellany), versus $10,000–$100,000 to hire it out, $5,000–$15,000 for a studio shoot, or ~$150,000 at Skullgirls' published 2D rate. **Total time: 4–10 weeks of solo work per character**, dominated by retiming.
- **The honest framing for the report:** AI does not make fighting-game animation cheap — *asset packs* already made the raw motion cheap, and AI makes *your own specific motion* cheap. What remains expensive in 2026 is exactly what was expensive in 2016: imposing gameplay-legal timing on motion, one clip at a time, by hand.
- **Second character onward is far cheaper** than the first in every route (rig, state machine, retime conventions, and a large fraction of reaction/locomotion clips all carry over) — which is the main reason the pack-plus-AI route scales to a shippable roster where a $150k/character pipeline does not.
